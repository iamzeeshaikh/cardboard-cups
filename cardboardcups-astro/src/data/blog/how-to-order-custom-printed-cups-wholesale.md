# How to Order Custom Printed Cups Wholesale

You have the logo files ready and a rough quantity in mind. What does a cup supplier actually need from you now. Buyers ordering custom printed cups wholesale for the first time often stall at exactly this point. The process is simpler than it looks once the steps sit in the right order. Artwork, cup selection, proofing, and scheduling each have a clear job. This guide covers what to prepare, what the supplier handles, and where orders usually go wrong.

## Planning a custom printed cups wholesale order

Three decisions shape every wholesale cup quote. The first is the cup itself, meaning size, wall type, and coating. The second is the print, meaning colors, coverage, and finish. The third is quantity, which drives unit cost more than any other factor.

Settle these in draft form before contacting anyone. A supplier quoting a vague request has to guess, and guessed quotes get revised later. A tight request gets a tight number instead. Write one paragraph describing the cup, the artwork, and the volume you expect over six months.

Timing belongs in the same paragraph. Note any launch date, event, or season the cups must arrive before. A supplier who knows the deadline can flag schedule risks in the first reply rather than the last one.

## Cup type and size

Start from the drinks the cups will hold every day. Hot programs typically need 8, 12, and 16 oz cups with matching lids. Cold programs lean toward 16 and 20 oz cups with straw or dome lids. An events business may add 4 oz sampling cups to the same order.

Wall construction matters for print as much as for heat. Single wall cups print on a smooth surface and cost the least. Double wall and corrugated cups add insulation but change how artwork wraps the outer layer. The [full cardboard cup range from Cardboard Cups](/) shows which constructions come in which sizes. Matching sizes across one rim family keeps lid stock simple, which staff will appreciate daily.

## Printing options

Cup decoration happens in one of two main ways. Flexographic printing applies ink to the board before the cup is formed. It suits large runs and solid brand colors at low unit cost. Offset-printed wraps deliver photographic detail and suit shorter runs with complex art. Your supplier will recommend one route once they see the artwork and quantity together.

Color counts drive cost in flexo work. A one-color logo on kraft board sits at the economical end. Full-coverage designs with four-color process printing sit at the premium end. Standard [printed cardboard coffee cups](/product/cardboard-coffee-cups/) handle both approaches well.

Think about where the eye lands during service. The strip facing the customer across the counter earns most of the attention. Many operators concentrate the design there and leave the rest calm. Finishes such as matte varnish exist, but most operators skip them since a cup lives for one drink.

## Artwork preparation

Suppliers work from a dieline, which is the flat template of your cup size. Ask for it before your designer starts on anything. Art built on the wrong template needs rework and delays the whole order. Each cup size has its own dieline, so a three-size order needs three prepared files.

A few file rules prevent nearly all prepress problems:

- Supply vector files for logos and text, not screenshots
- Convert fonts to outlines so nothing shifts on other systems
- Keep critical elements 5 mm clear of the seam and rim
- Extend background colors past the trim line as bleed
- Specify brand colors as Pantone references where exact matching matters

Remember that the seam overlaps a strip of the design. Place the logo opposite the seam so the join never crosses it. Low-contrast art on brown kraft also deserves a test, since the board tints every ink laid over it. When in doubt, ask the supplier to flag risky elements during prepress review. Most will mark seam collisions and thin lines without being asked twice.

## Proof and sample checks

Never approve a wholesale run from a screen preview alone. Screens show light, and cups show ink on board. The two rarely match exactly, and the gap widens on kraft stock.

Ask for a digital proof first to confirm layout and spelling. Then request a physical sample or press proof for color-critical work. Check the sample under the lighting of your actual counter. Hold it filled, lidded, and sleeved, exactly as a customer would.

White cup bases such as [white cardboard cups](/product/white-cardboard-cups/) show printed color most accurately, which makes sampling faster. Kraft bases mute cool colors and warm every tone slightly. Sign off in writing only when the physical item looks right in your own space.

## Quantity and lead time

Wholesale pricing works in quantity breaks rather than a single flat rate. Each threshold, often at carton or pallet multiples, drops the unit price a step. Ask where the breaks sit before you finalize your number. Moving an order up slightly to reach a break can cost almost nothing extra.

Custom printing adds production time that stock cups never need. Plate making, printing, forming, and freight each claim days on the calendar. Seasonal artwork needs ordering well ahead of the season it serves.

Storage is the quiet constraint in wholesale buying. A pallet of cups is bulky, so confirm you can hold what you save money buying. Split deliveries are worth asking about when space is short. Many suppliers will hold part of a run and release it on a schedule you set.

## Final order checklist

Run through this sequence before you release payment on the order.

1. Confirm the exact cup size, wall type, and coating in the order document.
2. Attach the approved artwork file and note the proof version signed off.
3. State ink colors with Pantone references where color matching matters.
4. Record the agreed quantity, price break, and any repeat-order terms.
5. Confirm the production window and the delivery address in writing.
6. Ask how reprints are handled if a defect appears in the delivered batch.

Keep a copy of every confirmation in one folder with the artwork. Repeat orders become five-minute tasks when the paper trail is complete. The reorder simply references the previous job number and any artwork changes. That habit alone saves more time than any other step in this guide.
